What do guests message about at night?

Guests message a villa at night about a short list of things: the air conditioning has stopped, the power is out, the driver cannot find the gate, they are locked out, there is no hot water, or they cannot get the wifi to work. Now and then it is something serious.

Because the list is short, the answers can be prepared. A villa does not need a night shift. It needs a plan that says who sees the message, how they judge it and who they call.

Sort every message into three levels

Every night message belongs to one of three levels, and the level decides who gets woken. Agree on the levels with your team in advance, so nobody has to judge it alone at 2am.

Most messages are the third kind, and most of those are answered by the house guide. The second kind is where reviews are won or lost: a guest lying in a hot bedroom at night is patient for about as long as it takes to feel ignored. Airbnb asks guests to rate communication as its own category, so a slow night shows up in the score.

Build the rota before you need it

A night rota is a written answer to one question: tonight, whose phone rings? Set it up once, before high season.

  1. Put every booking site into one inbox. A channel manager or shared inbox means one place to watch, not four apps.
  2. Name one person per night who sees guest messages, with a loud alert for that inbox only.
  3. Name one person on call near the villa who can be there fast, has keys, and is allowed to act.
  4. Write the call list: electrician, air conditioning technician, plumber, security, the nearest clinic with a night desk, and a driver. Test each number.
  5. Set a spending limit the on call person can approve without phoning the owner.
  6. Keep a log. Time of message, level, time of first reply, what was done. Read it monthly.

The log is what turns nights into improvements. If the same air conditioner appears three times, the fix is a technician in daylight, not a faster reply.

What the first reply should say

The first reply should do three things in three sentences: confirm you have read the problem, say what happens next, and say when the guest will hear from you again. It does not need to solve anything yet.

For a comfort level message it can be as short as this:

Example replyHi Sarah, thank you for telling us, and sorry about the air conditioning in the upstairs bedroom. Made from our team is on his way and will be with you in about 20 minutes. I will message you again as soon as he has looked at it.

Name the person who is coming and give a time. Then keep the promise in the last sentence, even if the news is "the part arrives at 9am, and we have set up a fan and the second bedroom for tonight".

Prevent the message in the first place

Many night messages are questions the arrival message should have answered. Each one is a small sign that something was unclear.

  • Cannot find the gate. Send a map pin that lands on the gate, a photo of it, and directions a driver can follow in the dark.
  • Late arrival. Explain how to get in after hours before the guest has to ask.
  • Wifi, air conditioning, hot water. One page in the house guide for each, with photos of the switches.
  • Power cut. Say what to expect, whether there is a generator, and where the torches are.

Who should answer: the owner, the staff or a manager?

The best person to answer at night is whoever can do both halves of the job: reply well, and get someone to the villa. In practice that is usually two people working together.

  • The owner knows the house best but is often in another time zone, and should not be the plan.
  • On site staff can act within minutes but need the authority to do so, and a night off.
  • A management or revenue team brings a process and languages, but still needs hands at the villa.

Questions owners ask

How fast should a villa reply at night?

As fast as it can acknowledge, which with a rota is a few minutes. Airbnb counts a reply within 24 hours toward the response rate it uses for Superhost, but a guest with a problem at night is not counting in hours. The first reply matters more than the fix.

Does a villa need someone awake all night?

No. It needs someone reachable. One phone with a loud alert for the guest inbox, and one person near the villa who will pick up a call, cover almost every case.

Should guests have a staff member's personal number?

Give guests one villa number or the booking site chat, not a personal phone. A shared number survives days off and staff changes, and it keeps every conversation where the rest of the team can read it.
